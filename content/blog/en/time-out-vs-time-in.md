# Time-Out or Time-In? What to Say at Ages 2–7

It's 5:40 p.m. Your toddler just threw a toy, or hit a sibling, or is past the edge of screaming. You're holding a baby with one hand and typing "time out vs time in toddler" with the other, wondering if sending them to their room makes you a bad parent, or if not doing it does.

Neither. Time-out is a short break from attention after a behavior that isn't okay, in a safe, quiet spot. Time-in means staying close, helping your child calm down, and naming the feeling before returning to the behavior. Under about age 3, start with redirection and staying close. If you use a time-out at all, keep it very short, consistent, and never in a scary room.

## What's the difference?

Neither tool is a "winner." Each has a place, and sources that support careful use of both.

| | Time-out | Time-in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The idea | A short break from attention and anything interesting, after a behavior that isn't okay | You stay close, help the child calm down, then come back to the behavior |
| Where you are | A separate, safe, quiet, boring spot (a chair, a step, not a bedroom) | Next to your child, at their eye level |
| The aim | Remove attention briefly so the behavior loses its payoff | Co-regulate first, teach second |
| Sources that support careful use | HealthyChildren/AAP, CDC, Nemours KidsHealth | Caring for Kids (CPS), Arizona PBS, PBS KIDS for Parents |

One note: the AAP also uses "time-in" for daily one-on-one play, and KidsHealth uses it for praise, different things. Here, "time-in" means only staying close during a hard moment.

## What authority sources actually say (no camp war)

This isn't a settled argument, more honest to say so than pick a side.

- **HealthyChildren (AAP):** warn once, name the behavior, move to a quiet place (not the bedroom), about a minute per year of age. From 3, "come back when ready" can replace a timer. Adults can use one too.
- **CDC:** reserve it for dangerous, harmful or rule-breaking behavior; consider time-in when the child is distressed, not defiant.
- **Nemours KidsHealth:** a kitchen chair works; 3–5 minutes is plenty at any age.
- **Arizona PBS:** leans toward redirection and praise, "for many toddlers, time-in works better."
- **Caring for Kids (CPS):** time-in as the go-to; no time-out under 3.
- **PBS KIDS for Parents (Claire Lerner, 2016):** a shaming time-out can backfire; under-3s can't yet "reflect" the way it assumes.

Where sources genuinely split is age 2: AAP, CDC and KidsHealth call a short time-out fine there; CPS and PBS lean toward time-in. Kidsit's position, no further than this: under about age 3, start with redirection and staying close. Use a short time-out only if at all, and only when the child is safe and can understand the limit.

## Exact words by age

**Ages 2–3** (the age-2 disagreement above, in practice):
- Stop: "I won't let you hit. Hitting hurts."
- Name: "You're so mad. I'm right here."
- Redirect: "Hands are for gentle touches." Or: "Let's stomp our feet together."
- Close: "All done. Let's sit and read one book."
- Skip: "why did you do that," forced sorry at the peak, a room alone.

**Ages 3–5** (logical consequences work now; "come back when ready" starts to apply):
- Limit + consequence: "No throwing blocks. If you throw them, they go on the shelf. Want to try again?"
- Feeling + alternative: "You're angry. You can stomp or squeeze this pillow. Hitting isn't okay."
- Calm-down spot: "Come back when you feel ready, and I'll be right here."
- After: "Next time you can say 'stop' or come get me."

**Ages 5–7** (HealthyChildren's cap is around 5 minutes here; no source gives wording this specific, so this script is Kidsit's own judgment):
- Pause: "I need us both to calm down. Let's take five minutes, then talk."
- After: "What happened? What were you feeling? What could you do differently?"
- Repair: "How can you make it right with your sister?", offer choices, don't force an apology.

## When your child hits a sibling

Your 3-year-old just swung a truck at their 5-year-old brother, who's crying, and you're holding a hot pot.

1. **Stop and protect.** Step between them, move the truck. "I won't let you hit."
2. **Check the hurt child first, briefly.** "Are you okay? Let me look." Skip "who started it."
3. **Read the child who hit.** Can they take comfort? If yes, time-in: "You're mad. I'm here. We don't hit." If they push away or kick, a calm break nearby, then check in.
4. **One short limit, calm voice.** "Hitting hurts. You can be mad. You can't hit."
5. **Repair once both are calm.** "Want to bring your brother a cold pack, or tell him you're sorry?" Offer a choice; don't force it.
6. **If both hit:** "Hurting is not okay for either of you. Everyone take a break."
7. **At bedtime:** a one-on-one minute with each child, no reopening the fight.

No birth-order explanation and no statistic promises this "stops it" for good, this is a way through the next ten minutes.

## How to do a time-in (words + body)

Get to your child's eye level, or sit next to them. If they're screaming, a quiet presence for 10–30 seconds does more than words. One sentence is enough: "I'm here. When you're ready, we'll breathe together." Wait until their body settles before asking them to fix anything or say sorry. Once the storm eases, teach the short alternative, "gentle hands look like this", and reconnect. No lecture, and no demanding "sorry" at the peak.

## If you choose a time-out

No lecture, arguing or eye contact during it (CDC). If your child gets up, walk them back quietly and restart the timer (HealthyChildren). When it ends: "Time-out is over. Let's try gentle hands."

**When not to use one:** infants; a child who can't yet connect action to result; a child who's fully flooded (time-in fits better there); or once it's a nightly power struggle rather than an occasional tool.

## A calm-down corner you share

This is where time-out and time-in actually meet: a place, not a punishment.

Set it up while everyone's calm, a rug, a pillow, a soft toy, a book. Frame it as "a place to calm our bodies," never "where you go when you're bad." Sit there with your child, especially under age 3; from 3 on, offer rather than force it: "Come back when you feel ready." If they can't accept comfort, a break nearby is fine, stay in sight, check in after a few minutes. Keep it safe: never a dark room, closet, or locked door. Not every child takes to a calm-down corner, that doesn't mean it failed; some just need you, not a spot.

## "I sent them to their room," "I yelled," "I locked the door": repair without shame

"I yelled and sent you to your room. That wasn't okay. It's fine to be mad, and it's not okay to hit. Next time I'll stay with you, we'll take a short time-out in the living room." If a door got locked and your child was scared, reconnect right away with the same repair line, and don't lock it again.

## A time-out for you

Genuinely official, HealthyChildren supports it for parents too. "I'm taking a minute. You're safe. I'll be back." Step out, calm down, then return for a hug.

## Afterwards: a short reconnect at bedtime

One or two minutes, and don't reopen the fight: "Today was hard with the anger. I saw that. I love you even when you're mad." Add one good thing from the day.
