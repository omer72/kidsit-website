# Time-out or time-in for a toddler? What to do instead of sending them to their room

They hit a sibling, or threw a toy, and now they're screaming. It's 5:40 p.m., after daycare or preschool, and you're wondering: send them to their room, sit them on a chair, or stay next to them. This doesn't mean you're a bad parent, and it doesn't mean the child is "bad."

Time-out is a short break from attention and from anything interesting after a behavior that isn't okay, in a safe, quiet spot. Time-in is the other direction: you stay next to your child, help them calm down, and name the feeling, then come back to the behavior. Under about age 3, start with redirection, a short limit and staying close. If you use a time-out at all, keep it very short, consistent, and never in a scary room. Below: what sources say, words for the moment, and what to do if you already sent them to their room.

## What's the difference?

Neither tool is a "winner" or a "loser." What fits depends on age, how flooded the child is, and what you're trying to teach. Some US materials use "time-in" for daily play or for praise; this page means stay close during a hard moment.

|  | Time-out | Time-in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The idea | A short break from attention and from anything interesting | Stay close, help the child calm down, and name the feeling |
| Where you are | A safe distance, no talking or eye contact (CDC / AAP) | Next to the child, at eye level, or a hug if they want one |
| The aim | Stop dangerous or hurtful behavior and teach a limit | Teach calming down through connection |
| Sources that support careful use | AAP/HealthyChildren; CDC; Nemours KidsHealth (around age 2) | Arizona PBS (for many toddlers, time-in may work better); CPS; CDC when the child is distressed |

## What authority sources actually say

[HealthyChildren (AAP), "How to Give a Time-Out"](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/Time-Outs-101.aspx) (last updated 11/5/2018): one warning, name the behavior, a quiet place that is not the bedroom or playroom, one minute per year of age, and walk the child back if they leave. From age 3 you can try "come back when you feel ready." Adults can take a time-out too, and time-outs "can be used too much."

The [CDC's "How to Use Time-Out"](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/time-out/steps.html) (30 April 2026): use it for dangerous or harmful behavior, breaking a family rule, or not following a direction after one warning; one minute per year of age; no talking, looking or playing during. A note there says time-out may not be best when the child is upset, and to consider time-in in emotional distress.

[Nemours KidsHealth, "Disciplining Your Toddler"](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/toddler-tantrums.html) (reviewed February 2023): a short time-out from positive attention for hitting, biting or throwing food, on a kitchen chair or the bottom stair; about one minute per year of age, and 3–5 minutes is plenty. Walk them back calmly if they get up, without talking. (That page uses "time-in" to mean praise for good behavior. This article does not.)

[Arizona PBS, "Positive discipline techniques for kids under 5"](https://azpbs.org/2026/11/positive-discipline-techniques-for-kids-under-5/) (3 November 2026): for toddlers, redirection, routines, transition warnings and praise; for many toddlers a time-in can teach more than sending them away alone. It does not ban time-out. [Caring for Kids (Canadian Paediatric Society)](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/behavior-and-development/positive-discipline-for-young-children) (updated January 2020) defines time-in as sitting and talking about feelings and behavior at the child's level, and says not to use time-out under age 3, and only after other positive methods.

[PBS KIDS for Parents, "Are Timeouts Helpful or Harmful to Young Children?"](https://www.pbs.org/parents/thrive/are-time-outs-helpful-or-harmful-to-young-children) (Claire Lerner, 14 January 2016): an angry, shaming time-out can backfire; a calm break can help when the child cannot accept comfort; under 3, don't expect them to "think about what they did."

The disagreement about age 2 is real: AAP, the CDC and KidsHealth describe a short time-out around age 2; CPS says not under 3; Arizona PBS prefers time-in for many toddlers. Kidsit does not pick a camp. Under about age 3, start with redirection, a short limit and staying close. Use a short time-out only if at all, and only when the child is safe and can understand the limit.

## Before you send them to their room: the 30 seconds that prevent half the fights

### Safety first

If they hit, throw, or run toward the street: block with your body, and move the dangerous object. One line: "I won't let you hit. It's OK to be mad."

### Name the feeling + a short limit

Even without a "formal" time-out or time-in:

- "You're mad the tower fell. We don't throw blocks at your brother."
- "It's hard to stop. I'm going to help you stop."

### Redirect

Offer what they can do, not only what they can't: "Blocks aren't for throwing. Let's build / bang a pillow / run in the hallway." Arizona PBS and Caring for Kids treat this as a first step, before anyone is sent away.

## How to do a time-in

1. Get down to eye level or sit next to them, not over them.
2. Quiet presence for 10–30 seconds if they're screaming too hard for words.
3. One sentence: "I'm here. When you're ready, we'll breathe together / we'll talk."
4. After the storm eases a little: "Hands hurt your sister. Gentle hands look like this." (model a soft touch)
5. No lecture. Don't demand "say sorry" at the peak.

Ready-to-say lines:

- "I'm not going anywhere. Let's sit here until your body settles."
- "You're mad, and I'm right here. No hitting."
- "When you're ready, we'll fix it together."

## If you choose a time-out: how to do it carefully

Only after redirection or a short limit, and only for a behavior you already named (hitting, biting, a clear family rule) — following the CDC, HealthyChildren and KidsHealth.

### The steps

1. One warning, if it isn't immediate danger: "If you keep hitting, it's time-out."
2. Name the behavior once: "Time-out because you hit."
3. A quiet, safe spot: a corner, a sturdy chair, or the end of the hallway — not a dark room, a closet, or "go to your room and play." HealthyChildren: not the bedroom or playroom. The CDC: no lights-off, never a closet; if you must use a bedroom, remove the toys and stay nearby.
4. One minute per year of age (age 2 ≈ 2 minutes). KidsHealth: 3–5 minutes is plenty; longer doesn't add benefit.
5. No lecture, no argument, no looking or talking during (CDC).
6. If they get up, walk them back quietly and restart the timer (HealthyChildren).
7. At the end: "Time-out is over. Let's try gentle hands." No speech about what they did.

### When not to

Not for infants, or for a child who doesn't yet understand action and result, or for a child so flooded they cannot "sit and think" — Arizona PBS and CPS prefer time-in then, and the CDC says to consider time-in when the child is distressed. And not when it becomes a long power struggle every evening (HealthyChildren: time-outs can be used too much).

## "I sent them to their room / I yelled / I locked the door" — repair without shame

Apologize for the tone or the scary isolation, not for the limit: "I yelled and sent you to your room. That wasn't okay. It's fine to be mad, and it's not okay to hit. Next time I'll stay with you / we'll take a short time-out in the living room." If a door was locked and the child was scared, reconnect right away and use the same repair. Don't lock the door again.

## A time-out for you

Yes, this is in the official guidance. Per HealthyChildren: if you're losing control, make sure the child is in a safe place, step out for a moment to calm down, then come back for a hug and a fresh start. A line for yourself: "I'm taking a minute. You're safe. I'll be back."

## Afterwards: a short reconnect at bedtime

One or two minutes at bedtime, without reopening the fight: "Today was hard with the anger. I saw that. I love you even when you're mad." Then one good thing from the day.

## When it's more than a hard moment

<aside class="blog-note" role="note">

Every parent has sent a child away or raised their voice. It's worth getting support if:

- hitting or blow-ups keep hurting someone, or they scare you, or they wear the household down;
- time-outs, yelling or fear are part of most days and you can't stop, or you're afraid of losing control (Caring for Kids: talk to someone before it affects the relationship);
- you're worried about your child's development.

No invented thresholds ("if it lasts X weeks").

</aside>

Where to turn:

- **Your child's pediatrician.** [Find a pediatrician on HealthyChildren (AAP)](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/find-pediatrician/Pages/Pediatrician-Referral-Service.aspx).
