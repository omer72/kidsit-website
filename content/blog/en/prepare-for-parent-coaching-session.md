# How to prepare for a parent coaching or child therapy session: what to note between sessions

Sessions are weekly, every other week, or monthly, and the week in between blurs. You sit down, get asked "how was it?", and all that's left is "hard," or just last night. That is not because you do not care. Details fade when the days are full.

To prepare for a parent coaching (parent guidance) session, bring two or three real moments from the past week: when and where it happened, what came right before, what your child did and what you did, and how it ended. Add what you tried and what helped, any changes in routine (sleep, illness, daycare or school), and one question you want answered. For a first session, bring relevant school or daycare notes too.

Below is a four-line template you can fill in two minutes the same evening, a short what-to-bring list, and how to open the session with one moment, not a list.

## Why the week is gone by session day (and it's not you)

The [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/how-to-work-well-with-your-childs-therapist/) notes that a therapist sees a child for one session a week, and that parents see the rest. What happens at home is most of the story, and it is easy to lose the details by the time you sit down.

Israel's [Ministry of Health, on its page about how therapy with children works](https://me.health.gov.il/mental-health/therapy-rehabilitation/public-care/kids-and-teens/kids-therapy-process/), makes a similar point: the child is with the therapist for about an hour a week and the rest of the time at home with parents, and parent work is an essential part of a child's therapy.

This page is not a guide to [which kind of help fits you](/en/blog/parent-coaching). It is for a parent who is already in a regular process and wants to arrive prepared.

## What to note between sessions

You do not need a clinical file. A few lines, the same evening, about one or two moments, is enough.

### A 4-line behavior log (two minutes, same evening)

For each hard moment, four lines. A phone note or a notebook is enough. The shape follows the behaviour diary from [Action for Children](https://parents.actionforchildren.org.uk/feelings-behaviour/understanding-emotions-behaviour/behaviour-diary/) (situation, behaviour, what happened next, and a short reflection), and the Child Mind Institute's suggestion to keep notes on how often, how intense, and how long a behavior lasts — in plain language, not a spreadsheet.

<div class="behavior-log">

1. **When and where** — "Tuesday, 7:40, at the daycare door."
2. **Right before** — tired, a transition, a "no", [screen time ending](/en/blog/screen-time-meltdown).
3. **What they did, what I did** — like a camera: "Lay on the floor and screamed about 10 minutes. I raised my voice."
4. **How it ended / what helped** — "I sat next to him; he calmed down."

</div>

Describe what you saw and heard, not labels. "Lay on the floor and screamed" tells more than "was manipulative."

### Note what worked, too

A quiet evening, a line that helped, a transition that went well. Action for Children says to record the positives too. Otherwise you arrive with only the hard moments, and the week sounds as if it was all like that.

### Session "homework"

One line: tried / didn't try / tried and X happened. "Didn't get to it" is information, not failure. Your professional needs to know what actually happened at home, including when you did not get to what you agreed to try.

### What you don't need to log

Not every moment. No labels. No identifying details about other children. Two or three moments a week usually give you something to talk about — that is an editorial suggestion, not a clinical rule.

### This isn't ABA data

If your child's BCBA or therapist gave you a specific sheet, use theirs. This log is for everyday hard moments, not clinical data collection.

## What to bring

- Two or three moments from the log, and the one that matters most to you.
- What you tried and what helped (Child Mind Institute).
- Changes: sleep, illness, daycare or school, a new sibling, a move.
- What daycare or school is seeing.
- One or two questions and one goal for this session.
- Documents only when relevant, mostly for a first session.

### Before a child therapist or psychologist appointment

Describe what brought you, when it started, where it happens and where it doesn't, and what you have already tried. You do not need to arrive with a diagnosis. If you are still asking whether this is coaching or therapy, see [which kind of help fits you](/en/blog/parent-coaching).

### When both parents come (or one)

Five minutes beforehand: which moment are we bringing today? That way the start of the session is not spent aligning, and a parent who comes alone can still choose in advance what matters most.

### Monthly sessions

If sessions are farther apart, last week alone does not tell the month. Kidsit's daily or weekly summary covers only today or the current week and is replaced when a new week starts; earlier summaries aren't saved. So:

1. **Once a week** (say, the last evening of the week), open the weekly summary, if it is turned on, and jot two or three lines: which situations kept coming up, what helped, and one moment "for the session." A phone note or a notebook is enough.
2. **Or**, before the session, scroll through History (the list of that child's moments) and pick two or three.
3. Bring the four weekly notes, not the whole month.

There is no monthly summary, and no archive of earlier summaries or a way to browse by date.

### Ask your professional what to track

On [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/health-management/Pages/making-the-most-of-your-childs-health-appointments-parent-tips.aspx), a page of parent tips for children's health appointments (last updated August 26, 2025), the American Academy of Pediatrics suggests bringing a list of questions, and asking whether there is information you should track before the next appointment. The same habit helps before a coaching or therapy session: if your professional wants a particular format, use theirs.

## How to use it in the session

1. Open with one moment, not a list: "I want to talk about Tuesday morning."
2. Read the four lines.
3. Say what helped and what didn't.
4. Ask: "What should I try next time this happens?"
5. Before leaving, write one line: what we're trying this week.

The Child Mind Institute notes that stories help, because a child may present differently in the office than at home. A short story from home gives your professional something to work with.

Your professional leads. If they want a different format, use theirs. This is only a way to arrive with what happened, not a way to run the session.

## When not to wait for the next session

If there is a safety concern, a sharp change, or a parent in distress, do not wait for the next session. Contact your treating professional or your child's pediatrician; in an emergency, local emergency services. See "When not to rely on any of these alone" on the page about [which kind of help fits you](/en/blog/parent-coaching). This page does not list phone numbers.
