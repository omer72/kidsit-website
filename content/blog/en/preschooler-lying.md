# My preschooler lies: what to say in the moment (without interrogating or punishing)

Marker on the wall, marker still in their hand, and "I didn't do it" right to your face. A lie at 3, 4 or 5 is common, and it is not a verdict on your child's character.

Lying at ages 3–5 is common and part of development. When you already know what happened, don't ask "Did you do it?" Say what you see ("I see juice on the floor"), move to fixing it together, and make the truth safe: "You won't get in trouble for telling me." Thank your child when they tell the truth, and avoid calling them a liar. Below: the first sentence, a short swap table, and how to fix the thing while keeping the truth safe to say.

## Why young children say things that aren't true

Self-control, and the line between pretend and real, are still developing — into about age 6, according to [HealthyChildren (AAP), "When Children Lie"](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/when-children-lie.aspx) (updated 18 March 2025), and [ZERO TO THREE, "When Young Children Lie"](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/when-young-children-lie/) (13 February 2017). Fear of your reaction is a main driver, ZERO TO THREE notes. The bigger our reaction, the more reason there is to lie next time.

[Raising Children Network, "Children & lying"](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/behaviour/common-concerns/lies) describes lying starting around ages 2–3 and becoming more common at 4–6, often once a child realises you can't read their mind.

### Pretend, a wish, or a cover-up

- **Pretend:** "A dragon ate my sandwich." Play along for a moment, then: "Is that a pretend story or a real one?"
- **A wish:** "Daddy said I can have a popsicle." Name the wish and keep the limit: "You really want a popsicle. That's after dinner."
- **A cover-up:** "I didn't spill it." State the fact and move to the fix, below.

## The first 10 seconds

1. **Breathe, and don't ask what you already know.** Skip "Did you do it?" — that's the advice from ZERO TO THREE and Raising Children.
2. **Say what you see, in a calm, flat voice:** "I see juice on the floor."
3. **Go straight to the fix:** "Here's a towel. Let's wipe it together."
4. **Leave a door open for the truth:** "If something happened, you can tell me. You won't get in trouble for telling me."

### Don't set a trap

When you already know, "Did you do it?" gives a young child both a chance to lie and a reason to. Stating the fact and moving to the repair leaves nothing to hide.

### When the truth comes

"Thank you for telling me. That was brave." And you still fix the thing. Raising Children advises praising the admission.

## Fix the thing, not the child

A small repair that matches what happened — wipe, rebuild, say sorry to a sibling — teaches more than "a week with no TV." HealthyChildren notes that harsh punishment is usually not effective. Never "liar": name the act, not the child, as [Child Mind Institute, "Why Do Kids Lie?"](https://childmind.org/article/why-kids-lie/) and Raising Children both stress. Your own honesty at home is the long game (HealthyChildren) — one line, not a lecture.

### If you already blew up

Repair it simply: "I yelled and called you a liar. That wasn't OK. I'm sorry. Let's fix this together." Repair teaches honesty, too. If the moment was a full-on meltdown rather than a fib, there is a separate guide: [screen-time meltdowns](/en/blog/screen-time-meltdown).

## Instead of this, say this

| Instead of this | Say this | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Did you do this?" (when you saw it) | "I see marker on the sofa. Let's get a wet cloth." | No trap, so nothing to lie about. You go to the fix. |
| "Liar!" / "You're lying to me!" | "That's not what happened. Let's try again." | You correct the fact without a label the child can grow into. |
| "Tell me the truth or no TV!" | "You won't get in trouble for telling me. We'll fix this together." | The truth becomes the safe option, not the dangerous one. |
| "Stop making things up!" (about a dragon) | "Is that a pretend story or a real one?" | You keep the pretend play and separate it from facts. |
| "Daddy didn't say that." | "You really want that. It's after dinner." | You name the wish behind the line and keep the limit. |
| "Why did you lie to me?" | "Thank you for telling me. That wasn't easy. Now let's fix it." | A 4-year-old often can't answer "why"; thanks for the truth is what lasts. |
| "Go to your room!" | "You spilled, so you help wipe." | A small repair tied to the act teaches more than a big punishment. |

If "Tell me the truth or no TV" is the sentence on your lips, there is also a guide to [what to say instead of threats](/en/blog/instead-of-threats).

### When it's more than a hard moment

Some lying at 2–6 is expected. It's worth talking to your pediatrician or a professional if:

- lying is constant and comes with other things that worry you (hurting others or animals, frequent aggression, or a big change in sleep, eating or play) — Raising Children, and HealthyChildren notes that persistent lying can be a sign that help is needed;
- your child seems afraid to tell you something, or you suspect they're covering for someone. Make it safe to tell you, and talk to a professional (Raising Children);
- you find yourselves in a daily fight about lying, and it's wearing you down.

**Where to turn:** your child's [pediatrician](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/find-pediatrician/Pages/Pediatrician-Referral-Service.aspx) (HealthyChildren find-a-pediatrician). For which kind of help fits you, see [parent coaching](/en/blog/parent-coaching). In an emergency, call 911 or your local emergency services.
