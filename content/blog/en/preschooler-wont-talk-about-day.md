# "What did you do today?" "Nothing": how to get your preschooler talking about their day

You ask every day, and every day it's "nothing". Meanwhile the preschool group chat shows painting, a birthday and a puppet show.

For a 3–5-year-old, "your day" is a huge question, and pickup is a tiring transition. Young children remember moments, not summaries, and often need to decompress before they can talk. It usually isn't secrecy or rejection. Ask something small and concrete, not at the door: "Who did you sit next to at breakfast?", "What did you play outside?", "What made you laugh?" Use a photo from gan if you have one, then wait. Below: when to ask, smaller questions, side-by-side moments, and what to say if something hard comes out.

## Why "nothing"

"Your day" is a huge, abstract question for a 3- or 4-year-old. They remember moments, not a summary of hours. Pickup is also a transition, and many children need to decompress first. If pickup ends in a meltdown rather than silence, that is a different moment — see [screen-time meltdowns](/en/blog/screen-time-meltdown) if the fight starts when a screen goes off. At this age, "nothing" is usually not secrecy or rejection.

## Not at the door: when to ask

Give a snack and some quiet first, then try later: in the car, in the bath, at dinner or at bedtime. [South Australia Department for Education, "What happened today?"](https://www.education.sa.gov.au/parents-and-families/curriculum-and-learning/early-years/learning-with-your-child-greatstart/word-play/what-happened-today) suggests making a routine for catching up — for example at the table or at bath time. [Raising Children Network, talking and listening at 3–6](https://raisingchildren.net.au/guides/activity-guides/letters-words-and-stories/talking-and-listening-activity-children-3-6-years) suggests a special time to talk, such as after getting into bed.

Give a few minutes of undivided attention, with screens off, as [HealthyChildren (AAP), "Improving Family Communications"](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/Improving-Family-Communications.aspx) (updated 15 December 2025) advises.

### Side by side

Kids often talk more while doing something next to you — driving, drawing, building — than face to face.

### Go first

Share a small piece of your day: "I spilled coffee on my shirt today. What was the silliest thing at gan?"

## Smaller questions

Kidsit's own short list, mixed with a little waiting:

- "Who did you sit next to at breakfast?"
- "What did you play outside?"
- "Was anything hard today?"
- "What made you laugh?"
- "Thumbs up or thumbs down day?"
- From the teacher's photos: "I saw you painted! What color did you pick?"

Mix open and closed questions, and wait for the answer, as the South Australia activity suggests.

## When something hard comes out

Listen first, and don't rush to fix it: "That sounds hard. What happened then?" Keep questions open rather than leading ("Tell me about outside time," not "Did someone hit you?"). Thank them: "I'm glad you told me." If it's about safety or someone hurting them, stay calm, take it seriously, and talk to the gan staff and, if needed, a professional — see the box at the end of this page.

## Instead of this, say this

| Instead of this | Say this | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "How was your day?" | "Who did you sit next to at breakfast?" | A small, concrete question a 4-year-old can answer. |
| "What did you do today?" (at the door) | (at the door) "I missed you. Snack?" … (later, in the bath) "What did you play outside?" | Timing: decompress first, talk later. |
| Five questions in a row in the car | One question, then quiet. Hum, drive, wait. | Space lets the answer come. |
| "Why do you never tell me anything?" | "You don't have to tell me now. I'm here at bedtime if you want." | No pressure, and the door stays open. |
| "Did someone hit you?" | "Tell me about outside time." | Open, not leading; you hear their version. |
| "Just ignore him." | "That sounds hard. What did you do then?" | Listen first; solve later, together. |
| "Nothing? You were there all day!" | "I saw you painted today! What color did you pick?" | A hook from the teacher's photos makes recall easier. |

### When silence is more than a phase

Most "nothing" is ordinary. It's worth checking with your pediatrician or the gan staff if:

- your child talks freely at home but doesn't speak at all at gan for more than a month after settling in (not counting the first month in a new place), and it gets in the way of playing or joining in — [ASHA on this pattern](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/disorders/selective-mutism/) (reviewed 1 May 2026) says to start by talking to your doctor;
- there's a sudden change: your child stops talking much at home, loses words they used to say, or seems withdrawn, fearful about going to gan, or has new sleep, eating or toileting changes that last;
- your child tells you something that worries you about gan or about an adult. Stay calm, thank them, and talk to the gan staff and a professional.

**Where to turn:** your child's [pediatrician](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/find-pediatrician/Pages/Pediatrician-Referral-Service.aspx) (HealthyChildren find-a-pediatrician). For which kind of help fits you, see [parent coaching](/en/blog/parent-coaching). In an emergency, call 911 or your local emergency services.
