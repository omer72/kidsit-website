# Toddler won't hug grandma: what to say to your child, and what to say to grandma

Grandma opens her arms, your toddler disappears behind your leg, and someone says "Don't be rude!" You're caught between a child who pulls away and a relative who is hurt.

You don't have to make your child hug or kiss relatives. Offer a choice of greeting ("Wave or high-five?"), and if they need time, let them stay with you and say hi when ready. Tell the relative warmly and briefly: "He's warming up, give him ten minutes." Skip bribes, and stop tickling when your child says stop. Below are both lines, a one-minute prep, and what to do about bribes and tickling.

## Do you have to make them?

No. [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/school-age/connecting-communicating/tough-topics/sexual-consent-how-to-talk-with-children-teens) says young children can be told it's their body, and they can decide who hugs, kisses or tickles them — and that they can say no. [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/why-you-shouldnt-force-the-kids-to-hug-granny-at-christmas-108059) (20 December 2018) and a pediatrician writing in [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-well-child/202512/doctors-orders-your-kids-dont-have-to-hug-anyone) (December 2025) agree: don't force it, and offer another way to say hello.

Shyness, a new place, noise, or not remembering the person since last time are ordinary reasons to pull back. Many warm up in 10–20 minutes — an editorial observation, not a statistic. Manners can still be a wave, a high-five, or saying hi.

## One minute before you go

A minute is enough.

> **To the child:** "Grandma is so happy we're coming. She might ask for a hug. You choose how to say hi: a hug, a high-five, or a wave."

> **To the relative** (a text or call the day before): "He's in a shy phase. We're letting him choose how to say hi. He loves you, he just needs a few minutes."

A choice often helps children relax. Telling grandma ahead of time makes the doorway less of a surprise.

## In the doorway: what to say to your child

When grandma leans in, offer a short choice, not an order.

> "How do you want to say hi to Grandma? Wave or high-five?"

If there's no response, keep it easy and move on:

> "You can say hi with words, or stay with me for now."

## What to say to grandma

Warm and short:

> "He's warming up. Give him ten minutes and he'll show you his new truck."

If they're hurt afterwards, acknowledge it without blame or a label on the child:

> "It's not about you. We're teaching him he gets to choose, and that it's OK to say no to anyone. When he does hug you, it'll be because he wants to."

A small tip: give the relative a role, not a demand — read a book, or help light the candles. That gives them a place without waiting for a hug at the door.

### Bribes and tickling

No treat in exchange for a kiss. That trade teaches that affection can be bought. Sufganiyot or cookies are for everyone.

If tickling or play continues after "stop," stop immediately: "He said stop, so we stop." Then a warm next step: "Want to show Grandma your truck?"

## Instead of this, say this

| Instead of this | Say this | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Give Grandma a kiss!" | "How do you want to say hi to Grandma: wave or high-five?" | A choice instead of an order; the child still greets. |
| "Don't be rude, she brought you a present!" | "Let's say thank you for the present together." | Manners through words, not through the body. |
| "You're hurting Grandma's feelings." | "You don't have to. You can say hi with words." | No guilt for a body boundary. |
| "One kiss and you get a sufganiya." | "You decide about kisses. Sufganiyot are for everyone." | Affection isn't a trade. |
| "Come on, just a little hug." | "It's OK. Stay with me, and say hi when you're ready." | Lets the child warm up instead of locking in. |
| (Grandma keeps tickling after "stop") | "He said stop, so we stop. Want to show Grandma your truck?" | Stop means stop, plus a warm next step. |
| (to Grandma) "Sorry, he's just being difficult." | (to Grandma) "He's warming up. Give him ten minutes." | Protects both: no label on the child, no blame on her. |

## When it's more than a hard moment

<aside class="blog-note" role="note">

Pulling away from hugs at family events is common. Take a closer look if:

- your child seems **frightened** of a particular adult (not just shy), or their behavior changes sharply around one person;
- your child tells you something about a person that worries you. Stay calm, thank them for telling you, and talk to your pediatrician or a professional;
- the shyness is extreme and constant everywhere (not just with relatives) and affects daily life. Worth raising with your pediatrician or Tipat Halav.

</aside>
